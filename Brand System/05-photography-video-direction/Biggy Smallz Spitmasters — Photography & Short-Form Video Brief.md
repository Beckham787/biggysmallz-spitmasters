# BIGGY SMALLZ SPITMASTERS
## Photography & Short-Form Video Brief

### Creative direction: FIRE & ATTITUDE

**Internal creative principle:**  
**Make it feel like Biggy Smallz just arrived.**

### The purpose of the content

Every photograph and video should make the viewer feel one or more of these things:

**Hungry. Curious. Wanting to be there.**

The content must sell the **experience**, not simply document catering.

Fire creates recognisability.  
Food creates appetite.  
The chef creates personality.  
People create warmth.  
Real moments create credibility.

The finished visual world should feel **bold, hot, human, generous, theatrical and confidently South African** — never staged, sterile or overly polished.

---

# 1. OVERALL PHOTOGRAPHY MOOD

Photography should feel:

**Immediate.**  
You feel like you are standing next to the fire.

**Tactile.**  
Smoke, char, juices, hands, heat, texture and movement should be visible.

**Human.**  
Guests should look like people enjoying themselves, not models posing for a catering brochure.

**Generous.**  
Full tables, large portions, shared food and abundance should be visible.

**Theatrical.**  
Fire, carving and serving are part of the show.

**Confident.**  
The photographs should have attitude without becoming macho, aggressive or gimmicky.

The visual benchmark is:

> **“This looks like the party I want to be at.”**

Avoid making every image “perfect”. Smoke in the frame, a moving hand, a messy table or slight motion blur can add authenticity. The approved direction explicitly prioritises **real over perfect**.

---

# 2. PREFERRED LIGHTING

## First choice: natural light

Use:

- early morning light
- late afternoon / golden-hour light
- side light
- backlight
- open shade
- window light

## Second choice: available event light

Use:

- firelight
- practical venue lighting
- warm evening light
- existing ambient lighting

## Strong preference

Look for **direction**, not brightness.

A side-lit piece of meat with visible texture is more valuable than a brightly lit but flat photograph.

Use:

**sunlight + shadow**  
**firelight + darkness**  
**backlight + smoke**  
**window light + food**

The approved direction specifically favours directional sunlight, firelight, flash, hard shadows, evening light and dramatic backlight, while avoiding generic bright-white restaurant aesthetics.

## Smartphone rule

Do not automatically use the brightest exposure.

Tap and lock exposure on the main subject. Slightly darker images are often preferable to washed-out highlights, particularly around fire.

---

# 3. PREFERRED CAMERA ANGLES

Use a mixture. Do not photograph the entire event from standing eye level.

### 1. Low angle

Use for:

- spit
- fire
- chef
- large pieces of meat
- dramatic serving moments

Makes the food and fire feel bigger.

### 2. Eye level

Use for:

- chef portraits
- guest interaction
- conversations
- authentic reactions

Creates intimacy.

### 3. Slightly above

Use for:

- tables
- platters
- shared food
- overhead serving moments

Shows abundance and composition.

### 4. Extreme close-up

Use for:

- crust
- carving
- flames
- smoke
- juices
- hands
- knives

### 5. Wide environmental

Use to establish:

- venue
- table
- crowd
- fire station
- chef within the event

The approved visual rhythm is:

**MACRO → PERSON → FOOD → FIRE → TABLE → CROWD**.

---

# 4. CLOSE-UP SUBJECTS

Prioritise details that make the viewer almost feel the food.

Capture:

- flames touching the meat
- glowing coals
- smoke
- fat rendering
- crisp crust
- char marks
- meat being sliced
- juices running
- sauce being poured
- seasoning being added
- hands carving
- hands serving
- knife cutting through meat
- steam rising
- food landing on plates
- fingers reaching for food
- half-eaten plates
- empty plates after the feast

**Food should look edible, not decorative.**

The approved direction specifically calls for crust, smoke, juices, char, texture, steam, carving, hands and serving.

---

# 5. WIDE-SHOT SUBJECTS

Every event should produce a few images showing the full experience.

Capture:

- the complete spit setup
- fire station in context
- chef working within the venue
- long tables
- guests seated together
- guests arriving
- full food spread
- venue + food + people
- dancing or celebration where appropriate
- sunset/evening atmosphere
- the room or outdoor setting when it contributes to the story

Wide shots should answer:

> **“What does it feel like to actually be at a Biggy Smallz event?”**

---

# 6. HUMAN MOMENTS TO CAPTURE

Human interaction is essential.

Look for:

- guests laughing
- someone seeing the food for the first time
- guests watching the spit turn
- someone tasting food
- a genuine reaction after tasting
- friends talking over the table
- hands passing plates
- guests reaching across the table
- someone pouring a drink
- children or families interacting where appropriate
- chef talking to guests
- chef joking with guests
- chef explaining food
- chef laughing
- guests taking photographs of the food
- the table becoming progressively messier

Do not instruct every person to smile at the camera.

**Observe first. Direct only when necessary.**

Guest reactions can be more valuable to the brand than another food close-up.

# 8. CHEF PORTRAIT DIRECTION

The chef must feel like a **character**, not a corporate catering employee.

Do not default to:

> Arms folded + chef jacket + neutral expression.

Instead capture:

- chef working
- chef standing beside the fire
- chef looking directly at camera
- chef laughing
- chef carving
- chef holding a platter
- chef tasting
- chef talking to guests
- chef with smoke/fire behind him
- chef mid-action
- chef between moments of service

### Portrait composition

Create three types:

**Hero portrait:**  
Chef facing camera, fire or event environment behind him.

**Working portrait:**  
Chef caught in action.

**Personality portrait:**  
Chef laughing, joking, reacting or interacting with someone.

The approved direction specifically calls for the chef to be photographed **looking at camera, working, laughing, carving, sweating, talking to guests, holding food and interacting with the fire**.

---

# 9. GUEST & EVENT MOMENTS

Do not document an event as if it were a formal conference.

Look for **energy**.

Prioritise:

- arrival
- first sight of the fire
- food being unveiled
- guests gathering around the cooking
- reactions
- conversations
- communal eating
- laughter
- celebrations
- speeches only when visually interesting
- dancing where relevant
- people lingering after the meal

For weddings and private events especially, capture the emotional progression:

**ARRIVE → DISCOVER → GATHER → EAT → CELEBRATE**

The event should still feel like a Biggy Smallz experience even when the venue, dress code and audience are more formal.

---

# 10. EQUIPMENT & SETUP SHOTS

Equipment photographs should not become technical product photography.

Capture equipment only when it helps communicate the **scale, craft and theatre** of the experience.

Useful shots:

- full spit
- meat on spit
- fire pit
- coals
- knives
- carving board
- serving platters
- preparation surfaces
- chef's hands using equipment
- loading/unloading where visually useful
- setup before guests arrive
- finished setup immediately before service

Avoid making the brand look like it sells grills and hardware.

The hero remains:

**Food + fire + chef + people.**

# 11. TEN ESSENTIAL PHOTOGRAPHS FOR THE BRAND LIBRARY

Every major event should aim to produce these ten images.

### 01 — THE HERO FIRE
A dramatic fire/spit image showing heat, smoke and movement.

### 02 — THE HERO FOOD
One irresistible close-up of finished food with texture, char and appetite appeal.

### 03 — THE CARVE
The chef actively carving or serving.

### 04 — THE CHEF
A strong personality-led portrait with the fire or event environment.

### 05 — THE REACTION
A genuine guest reaction to the food.

### 06 — THE TABLE
A strong wide shot showing abundance and people together.

### 07 — THE HANDS
Hands serving, passing or reaching for food.

### 08 — THE CROWD
A wider image showing the atmosphere and scale of the gathering.

### 09 — THE DETAIL
One highly tactile macro image: smoke, crust, flames, knife, juices or seasoning.

### 10 — THE AFTERMATH
A real post-feast image: empty plates, reduced platters, people still talking or the final fire.

These ten should become the minimum brand-library target for every important event.

### 03 — MEAT

### 04 — SMOKE

### 05 — CHEF
Chef looking at camera, working or laughing.

### 06 — KNIFE
Knife cutting or carving.

### 07 — SERVE
Food being plated or served.

### 08 — REACTION
Guest tasting and reacting.

### 09 — TABLE

# 13. FIVE RECURRING REEL FORMATS

## REEL 01 — THE FIRE IS ON

**Sequence:**

Cold setup → fire → spit → smoke → meat → chef → finished food.

Text:

**THE FIRE IS ON.**

Use for regular event documentation.

---

## REEL 02 — GET IN MY BELLY

Start with an extreme food close-up.

Cut rapidly through:

Food → carving → fire → chef → reaction → table.

End:

**GET IN MY BELLY.**

This is the primary brand-building format.

The approved direction positions “GET IN MY BELLY.” as a central visual brand asset rather than merely a tagline.

---

## REEL 03 — WHO'S COMING HUNGRY?

Start with chef looking at camera.

Text:

**WHO'S COMING HUNGRY?**

Then:

Fire → food → guests → reaction → full table.

Use for weddings, birthdays, corporate events, festivals and private dining.

---

## REEL 04 — FROM FIRE TO FEAST

Use the transformation story:

**FIRE → COOK → CARVE → SERVE → FEAST**

This format is especially useful when an event has strong food preparation footage.

---

## REEL 05 — THE BIGGY MOMENT

One human moment becomes the hero.

For example:

A guest takes a bite.

Reaction.

Everyone laughs.

Cut to chef.

Cut to fire.

End:

**MAKE IT A BIGGY ONE.**

The brand should not become an endless sequence of food montages; emotional moments should regularly carry the storytelling.

# 15. WHAT NOT TO PHOTOGRAPH

### Do not photograph:

- fake staged reactions
- people pretending to eat
- stock-looking posed groups
- generic “chef arms crossed” portraits
- food that has been heavily manipulated or digitally enhanced
- empty tables as hero images
- random venue architecture with no Biggy Smallz connection
- dirty prep areas unless deliberately documentary
- unflattering private moments
- identifiable guests looking uncomfortable
- competitors' branding as a dominant feature
- equipment as the main subject
- generic BBQ imagery
- anything that makes the business look like a hardware/grill company

### Avoid visual clichés

No:

- cartoon flames
- smoking skulls
- BBQ badges
- fake Americana
- animal-print “African” styling
- safari imagery
- fake tribal patterns
- black-and-gold luxury clichés
- generic corporate chef imagery

The approved direction specifically calls for avoiding these categories.

And most importantly:

> **Do not make Biggy Smallz look like someone else's idea of a braai brand.**

# 16. HOW TO KEEP VISUALS CONSISTENT ACROSS DIFFERENT EVENTS

The venues will change.

The clients will change.

The weather will change.

The food will change.

The visual language should not.

## Always capture the same five things

### 1. FIRE
Every event needs fire.

### 2. FOOD
Every event needs tactile food detail.

### 3. CHEF
Every event needs personality.

### 4. PEOPLE
Every event needs real human interaction.

### 5. REACTION
Every event needs evidence that people are enjoying themselves.

This creates consistency without making every event look identical.

---

## Keep the same visual rhythm

Across every shoot, aim for:

**MACRO → PERSON → FOOD → FIRE → TABLE → CROWD**

This is more important than applying the exact same editing preset to every photograph.

---

## Keep the same photographic behaviour

Prefer:

- strong directional light
- tight crops
- environmental portraits
- low angles
- real reactions
- movement
- imperfect moments
- genuine food texture
- dramatic fire
- human interaction

Avoid:

- flat lighting
- excessive retouching
- over-staged compositions
- sterile white backgrounds
- endless overhead food shots

# SMARTPHONE PRODUCTION RULES

The brand does not need a professional cinema camera to look professional.

A modern smartphone is enough when the person shooting understands **light, composition and timing**.

## Shoot vertically by default

Use:

**9:16**

for most video and social-first photography.

Take some horizontal photographs for:

- website hero sections
- website banners
- proposals
- print
- press

## Clean the lens

Before every event.

A dirty smartphone lens can make excellent food photography look cheap.

## Move your body before using digital zoom

Get closer where possible.

Avoid excessive digital zoom.

## Lock focus/exposure

Particularly around:

- fire
- bright sunlight
- reflective serving platters
- pale wedding clothing

## Hold shots slightly longer than necessary

For video, record an extra few seconds before and after the action.

This gives the editor usable footage.

## Capture natural sound

Record:

- fire crackling
- knife carving
- spit turning
- food sizzling
- guests laughing
- chef talking
- applause

Natural sound is useful because the approved direction specifically calls for natural sound alongside music.

# THE BIGGY CONTENT CHECK

Before approving an image or Reel, ask:

**Does it make me hungry?**

**Does it make me curious?**

**Does it make me want to be there?**

The approved social strategy says every content piece should ideally achieve at least two of those three outcomes.

If it does none of them, don't use it.

---

# THE NON-NEGOTIABLE RULE

**Only real Biggy Smallz food, real Biggy Smallz events and real Biggy Smallz people should be used as proof of the brand.**

AI-generated imagery may be useful for internal creative exploration or moodboarding, but it should **never be presented as a real event, real client, real dish or evidence of the business's work**.

The strongest asset Biggy Smallz can build is not a perfectly produced image.

It is an archive of **real fire, real food, real people and real occasions** that becomes increasingly recognisable over time.
